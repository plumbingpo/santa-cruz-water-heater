How San Jose Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is routine maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever once a year to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on descaling once a year if their water is hard.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters last 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models typically last 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing warning signs such as discolored hot water, rumbling during heating, dampness under the tank, or showers that go cold sooner, a planned replacement is nearly always less expensive and much easier on you than an emergency one after the tank lets go.

The sooner you call about a hot water problem, the smaller the repair tends to be. A tank that's just begun to rumble needs only a flush, yet the same sediment, ignored for a few years, can create hot spots on the tank floor and shorten the life of the tank, forcing a replacement much sooner. Save our number on your phone now so help is one call away the moment something seems off. A quick call saves time, money, and ruined floors.

What Goes Wrong With Local Water Heaters

The majority of water heater problems we see in San Jose come back to the water itself. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, minerals can be a real factor: calcium and magnesium settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, and that's what causes the popping and rumbling many homeowners hear when the heater fires. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling matters so much in hard-water areas.

Equipment age plays a big role too. Once the sacrificial anode rod wears out, often well before the tank itself looks old, the steel begins corroding from within, and a slow leak at the base is usually the first visible sign. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: pilot assemblies, gas valves, and flues on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Code requirements add another layer. Replacing an older heater frequently means updating the installation along the way: a thermal expansion tank if the water system is closed, a relief valve drain line that terminates correctly, a flue that drafts correctly on gas units, plus straps and a pan where required. Well water brings its own challenges, including extra sediment, iron staining, and the rotten-egg smell that sometimes shows up when certain water chemistry reacts with a standard anode rod. All of these can be fixed, and most are much cheaper to address early.
